Hinges, pivots and closers are what actually carry a door, and on a fire door they are what makes it a fire door at all. Ontario's Fire Code requires the self-closing devices on fire separations to be maintained in operating condition, and the door to close and latch on its own without anyone touching it — never wedged, propped or blocked. In Milton that covers the stairwells and corridor doors in every warehouse and plaza, the vestibules in the Derry Green office blocks, and the common-area doors in the town's stacked townhouse and apartment stock. A propped door or a closer that stalls short of the strike is one of the most common things an inspector writes up.

We replace continuous hinges, butt hinges, offset and centre pivots, and surface, concealed and floor closers, sizing the closer for the door, the swing and the real pressure at that opening — which in a warehouse running rooftop units against open dock doors is a genuine factor. Then we set sweep speed, latch speed and backcheck for cold-weather operation, so the same door still closes and latches when it is well below zero and the fluid has thickened. If traffic flow is why a fire door keeps getting propped, the correct answer is a hold-open device tied to the fire alarm, not a wedge.

Hinges, Pivots & Fire-Door Closers — your questions

Our fire door will not latch on its own — how urgent is that in Milton?

Treat it as urgent. Ontario's Fire Code requires closers on fire separations to be kept in operating condition so the door closes and latches unaided, and a door that is propped or does not latch is a serious finding on an inspection. We attend same-day for fire-door faults across Milton and Halton.

Can we keep the warehouse fire door propped open during a shift?

No. Propping or wedging a fire door defeats the separation it exists to create, and it is one of the most common violations written up in Milton commercial buildings. If traffic flow is the problem, the proper fix is a magnetic hold-open device tied into the fire alarm, so the door stands open for the shift and releases automatically on alarm.

Why does our closer work fine in summer and badly in January?

Hydraulic fluid thickens as it gets colder, so the closer slows and often leaves the door short of the strike. The right answer is seasonal adjustment of sweep, latch speed and backcheck — not winding the spring up so hard that nobody can pull the door open, which on an accessible entrance creates a different problem entirely.
